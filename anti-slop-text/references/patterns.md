# Common AI-slop patterns: a practical diagnostic reference

"AI slop" is an informal label, not a scientific category. These patterns are editorial warning signs; none proves that a passage was written by AI. Use judgment and context.

## 1. Generic openings

**Weak:** "In today's fast-paced and ever-changing world, technology plays a crucial role in our lives."

Why it underperforms: It delays the subject and offers no information specific to the topic.

**Better:** "Many schools now use online platforms to distribute assignments and track submissions."

Use the second version only when it is true for the context. Do not invent facts just to be specific.

## 2. Empty importance claims

**Weak:** "This is an incredibly important and significant issue that cannot be ignored."

Why it underperforms: It declares importance without explaining the stakes.

**Better:** State who is affected, what changes, or what consequence matters. If the stakes are unknown, remove the claim.

## 3. Repeated thesis

A paragraph says X, the next says X is important, and the conclusion says X matters. Combine or differentiate them: define the claim, support it, then explain its implication.

## 4. Canned transitions

Phrases like "Furthermore," "Moreover," "It is important to note," and "In conclusion" are not inherently wrong. They become slop when inserted mechanically, especially when the relationship between ideas is already obvious.

Delete the transition if the next sentence connects naturally. Keep it when it genuinely clarifies contrast, sequence, cause, or emphasis.

## 5. Formulaic contrasts

"Not only X, but also Y" and "not just X, but Y" can be useful. They feel hollow when Y merely restates X or when the contrast is manufactured for drama.

State the actual relationship directly.

## 6. Inflated praise and certainty

Watch for "groundbreaking," "revolutionary," "seamless," "unparalleled," "ultimate," "perfect," "guaranteed," and similar words. Keep them only when evidence and context justify them.

Replace unsupported superlatives with a measurable property or a narrower claim. Never convert a limited benefit into a universal promise.

## 7. Abstract language hiding the point

**Weak:** "The implementation of the solution facilitates the improvement of productivity."

**Better:** "The tool helps staff finish routine reports faster."

The second is clearer if that is what the evidence supports.

## 8. Over-engineered structure

Warning signs include a heading every few sentences, lists where prose would be easier, repeated three-part lists, and paragraphs that all have the same length. Structure should follow the material, not an aesthetic formula.

## 9. Synthetic conversational tone

Forced "Let's dive in," "Great question!", canned empathy, overdone enthusiasm, or slang added to every line can feel as artificial as stiff formal prose. Match the user's actual tone and the task.

## 10. Artificial humanization

Do not add typos, fake anecdotes, fake preferences, random fragments, or unearned first-person statements to make text look human. Authenticity comes from accurate details, genuine reasoning, and a voice that fits the author.

## 11. Overuse of punctuation and formatting

Repeated em dashes, bolding ordinary phrases, excessive parentheses, and dramatic colons can distract. Edit only where they obstruct flow; do not impose a punctuation ban.

## 12. False specificity

Invented statistics, named examples, quotations, citations, or personal experiences are worse than generic prose. If a detail cannot be verified or supplied, qualify the claim, remove it, or ask for evidence.

## 13. Uniform sentence rhythm

If every sentence has similar length and structure, revise for clarity and emphasis. Do not force random short sentences into the text; vary rhythm where the ideas naturally differ.

## 14. Stock endings

A conclusion should answer the question, state the practical implication, or leave the reader with a relevant next step. If the last paragraph merely repeats the opening in grander language, cut it.

## 15. Detector-score fixation

No wording recipe can guarantee that a detector will classify text as human-written. Detector outputs can produce false positives and vary by tool, genre, language, and sample length. Judge quality through evidence, specificity, coherence, and the author's actual contribution—not a claimed detector score.

## A quick test

For each suspicious sentence, ask:
1. What information does this sentence add?
2. Could it fit almost any topic?
3. Is its claim supported by the provided facts?
4. Is the wording more elaborate than the idea?
5. Would a real reader benefit from keeping it?

Rewrite only when the answer reveals a real problem.
